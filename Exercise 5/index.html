Four ways to look at energy

Each question about television energy use suits a different kind of chart. This page uses a scatter plot, a donut chart, a bar chart and a line chart, all drawn with D3.js from the course datasets.

Scatter plot

Do more stars mean less energy?

Yes, on average. Each dot is one of 4,233 televisions, and the dark line joins the average at each rating. One-star models average 1,243 kWh a year, while six-star models average 300 kWh. The relationship is weaker than for screen size (r = −0.50), because a large television can earn many stars and still use more energy than a small, low-rated one.

Figure 5.1: Energy consumption against star rating. Dots are spread slightly sideways so overlapping models stay visible; hover for details.

Donut chart and bar chart

Which screen technology uses the most?

The answer depends on how the question is asked. Across every television, LED accounts for 85% of the combined energy use, but mostly because 82% of the models are LED. Comparing only 55-inch models removes the effect of screen size, and the gap between technologies shrinks to about 13%.

Line chart

Electricity has become more expensive to buy.

Wholesale spot prices stayed between about $30 and $65 per megawatt hour from 1998 to 2015. Since then they have climbed sharply, peaking at an average of $144.50 in 2022, which makes every kilowatt hour a television uses more costly to supply.

Why these charts

01

Scatter plot for relationships

Plotting every model shows both the overall trend and how much individual televisions vary around it.

02

Donut chart for parts of a whole

Total energy use splits cleanly into three technologies, and with only three slices the shares stay easy to compare.

03

Bar chart for comparing values

Bars that share a zero baseline make small differences in average energy use easy to judge by length.

04

Line chart for change over time

Connecting each year to the next makes the long flat period and the recent rise stand out at a glance.
